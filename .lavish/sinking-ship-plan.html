Sinking Ship — last one dry wins

A battle royale on a ship that is going down. Eight brawlers on a small coastal steamer, no loot and no weapons: you shove, you brace, you push people into the sea. The rising water and the listing deck are the shrinking zone. The steamer sinks by the head, so the bow goes under first, the stern climbs, the decks tilt until standing still is work, and the last one standing on dry planks wins. You play it in first person, through your own brawler's eyes, and the steamer has an inside — a hold, an engine room, cabins and a saloon — that floods first and drives everyone upward.

The one thing this plan cannot get wrong is the shape of the simulation, because it is the one thing that cannot be retrofitted: bots now, online later, without a rewrite. The answer (§2) is a Node-free, 30 Hz simulation in core/ that Godot only draws — the same input → validate → apply → typed events → animate split Grid Commanders keeps, at tick granularity. Fifteen milestones make the MVP — first playable at SH1, a full single-player game at SH10, online at SH12, art last — and you approved them as the MVP on 2026-10-02. Eight more (SH16–SH23) lead to the destination you named: a ship as big as the Titanic that can go down by any side at any time.

The game in one page

The loop

  1. Eight brawlers spawn around the main deck. A three-second countdown, then the horn.
  2. Everyone shoves everyone. A shove is a short, committed push; a body knocked into the sea is out (§5 for exactly when).
  3. The ship goes down by the head on a fixed script: the bow floods, the deck lists, the stern rises, and every few seconds the dry area is smaller and steeper.
  4. Late on, standing still on a tilted deck is work — walk uphill or brace, or slide toward the water. Lurches throw everyone toward one rail; the highest perch gives way.
  5. The last one dry wins. Results, rematch, another seed.

What a three-minute match feels like

  • 0:00–0:45 · Calm. Fights on every level, from the hold to the bridge; the ship barely moves.
  • 0:45–1:30 · The flooding below. The sea pours into the hold at the bow and chases everyone aft and up the stairs; the companionways are the fight.
  • 1:30–2:40 · By the head, then the list. The forward main deck and the forecastle go under, the saloon floods, the deck lists one way then the other; the wheelhouse roof collapses.
  • 2:40–3:30 · The plunge. The stern stands up out of the sea, the decks are steeper than anyone can stand on for free, and the last two or three fight on the poop deck while it slides under.

What the player does

  • Look (rev 3) — first person: the mouse or the right stick turns you, and you shove where you look.
  • Move — analog, relative to where you look; Space jumps (SH4b).
  • Shove — tap: quick and weak; hold (from SH4): a charged shove that breaks a brace.
  • Brace (from SH4) — plant your feet: shrugs off a shove from the front and holds you on a steep deck, at a stamina cost.
  • Nothing else. No health, no items, no pickups. The water is the only way out.

The central decision — who owns where a body is

A real-time 3D brawl is not a turn-based grid, so Grid Commanders' architecture does not transfer for free. The question is who answers "where is every body, and what touched what, this tick". There are two serious answers. (a) A custom Node-free fixed-tick kinematic simulation in core/: characters as circles on deck surfaces, shoves as impulses, the ship as a handful of tilting platforms, the water as a plane, and Godot nodes drawing interpolated state. (b) Godot physics — CharacterBody3D on Jolt, which became the default 3D physics engine for new projects in Godot 4.6 — running on a headless authoritative server later.

Criterion(a) Custom kinematic sim in core/(b) Godot physics (CharacterBody3D, Jolt)
Tested under GUT without a scene tree Yes. MatchSim.step(frames) is a function call: a test builds a state, steps thirty ticks, asserts. Thousands of ticks a second, headless, in the scaffold's existing make test. No. Bodies need a SceneTree and a physics space; a test awaits real physics frames, and collision results depend on the engine's step. It also fights the scaffold's own rule that core/ holds no Node.
Determinism Ours to keep: seeded streams, a fixed processing order, no clock. Same build + same seed + same inputs ⇒ the same match, checked against a golden digest from SH1. Jolt is built to be deterministic for identical inputs in identical order, but the engine owns when and in what order bodies step. Nothing gives a test or a server a seed-and-replay contract over a whole scene.
Becoming server-authoritative Direct. The server runs the same MatchRunner as the offline game; it needs no scene at all, so a headless room is a few hundred kilobytes of state. Feasible: a headless Godot runs the full scene, and MultiplayerSynchronizer replicates transforms.
Client prediction Reset to the last snapshot and re-run MatchSim.step over the inputs the server has not acknowledged — cheap at eight bodies, and the same code as the server's. The engine offers no snapshot/restore of a physics world. Prediction means replaying move_and_slide for your own body against others that cannot be rewound — or no prediction, i.e. a full round trip of delay on your own movement in a game about timing.
Brawl feel Every number is ours: acceleration, friction, knockback, slide, the railing rule. The cost is that ragdolls, tumbling crates and stacking debris cannot matter to the rules; they become presentation, or our own simple bodies (SH10's crates). Tumbling, ragdolls and stacking for free — and the classic problem that comes with them: physics chaos is hard to tune, hard to read on screen and hard to network.
Build effort, part-time, through agents Roughly 1–2k lines of plain geometry by SH3, all test-first — the kind of work an agent can verify on its own, because GUT is the evidence. A faster first evening, then slower every week after: most behaviour can only be verified by a human playing, and the online milestone turns into a rewrite.
Level authoring The ship is a .tres of rectangles, ramps and upright cylinders (D6); the greybox is generated from it and the art must align to it. Paint colliders in the editor; any mesh can be ground.
Web export Fine, and the server never renders. Fine on the client; the server carries the whole scene.
Verdict Recommended. Rejected for gameplay; allowed for presentation-only effects.

Recommendation: (a). Every rule of the match — movement, slopes, railings, shoves, water, eliminations, the verdict — lives in RefCounted classes under core/ that advance in fixed 30 Hz ticks from one InputFrame per seat. Godot nodes only read snapshots and interpolate between the last two, so the offline game, the headless tools, the tests and a future server all run the exact same code. It is the only option where "becomes server-authoritative with client prediction" is a transport problem rather than a rewrite, and where an agent can prove a behaviour without a human at the keyboard.

What (a) rules out

  • Physics-driven bodies in gameplay — ragdolls, tumbling crates, debris that the rules read. A body the sim does not own cannot be predicted, replayed or made authoritative.
  • Collision from meshes or editor-painted colliders, and arbitrary 3D terrain. The ship is axis-aligned rectangles, ramps and upright cylinders in ship space (D6); a curved hull is drawn, not collided.
  • Wall-climbing and full 3D capsule contact (D8).
  • Jolt or GodotPhysics3D anywhere on the server.
  • Lockstep and rollback netcode (D11). Not ruled out by (a) itself — (a) makes them possible — but by D4's choice not to chase cross-platform bit-identical floats.

What (a) keeps

  • Godot physics for pixels. A ragdoll for an eliminated brawler, a splash, falling sparks, a flapping flag — anything the sim never reads back. That rule is D12.
  • The Grid Commanders split, intact. Input → validate → apply → typed events → animate; the AI issues the same input as a player; seeded RNG; a golden replay; GUT over the Node-free layers.
  • The editor. Scenes, the HUD, the camera, materials and the art pass are ordinary Godot. Only the rules moved out.
  • Web export. gl_compatibility is the one renderer the web platform supports, and nothing in the sim depends on threads.

Sources: Godot 4.6 release notes — Jolt is the default physics engine for new 3D projects, existing projects unaffected (godotengine.org/releases/4.6). Godot 4.7 docs, Exporting for the Web — web exports support only the Compatibility renderer; low-level networking is not implemented; only HTTP, WebSocket (client) and WebRTC are available; single-threaded export is the default (docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/…/exporting_for_web).

D1 · The match is a Node-free, fixed-tick simulation in core/; Godot only draws it

locked

All match rules live in RefCounted classes under core/ with no Node, scene, physics body or engine clock. Scenes read snapshots and interpolate between two ticks; no scene ever moves a character itself. The scaffold's tools/check_scripts.sh already refuses a Node, a scene, the tree and the global RNG under core/ and ai/; SH1 adds the engine's clocks and devices to the same refusal — Time., OS., Engine., Input., DisplayServer. — so the rule is enforced rather than remembered.

Rejected: Godot physics for gameplay (§2); and a hybrid in which Godot physics resolves collisions while core/ resolves rules — two authorities over where a body is, which is the drift every later milestone would pay for.

D2 · 30 Hz, and the tick is the only clock

locked

The sim advances in fixed steps of 1/30 s. Nothing in core/ or ai/ takes a delta or reads a time; a duration is a count of ticks. Ticks.RATE = 30 is a code constant, not a tuning value: changing it moves every timing, the wire format and the golden. Data is authored in seconds and metres for humans and converted once per match by Ticks.from_seconds (round half away from zero), the single conversion authority. Why 30 and not 60: half the GDScript cost for the sim, the bots and the client's re-simulation, half the snapshot bandwidth out of a home connection, and a shove's windup (3 ticks, 100 ms) still reads. Rendering runs at the display rate by interpolating the last two ticks, so 30 Hz never shows as stutter.

Rejected: stepping inside _physics_process with physics_ticks_per_second = 30. It ties the sim's rate to an engine setting and makes the game, the headless tool, the tests and the server step differently. One accumulator, in SimDriver, decides when the scene steps; every step, in every host, goes through MatchRunner.

D3 · One door in: an InputFrame per seat per tick

locked

Every seat — the local human, a bot, a remote player, a replay — produces exactly one InputFrame per tick, and MatchSim.step(frames) is the only way anything happens. The frame is quantized when it is created (move as integers −127…127 per axis, the look yaw as 0…65 535 for one turn), so an offline match and the same match over a wire see bit-identical inputs. Buttons are held state; presses and releases are derived inside the sim from the previous tick's buttons, so a lost packet cannot eat a press once redundancy restores the frame. The sim validates every frame — clamps it, ignores buttons from an eliminated or staggered seat — because a frame is a request, never a fact. The sim never learns which seats are human, where the camera is, or what device sent a frame. Move is expressed in the ship's horizontal plane. Since rev 3 the game is first person (D14): the frame also carries the seat's look_yaw — the facing it chooses this tick, quantized to 1/65 536 of a turn — and InputFrame.from_screen(stick, look_yaw) turns the stick into a move relative to that look (stick up walks where you look), computed against the quantized yaw so both ends of a wire agree. The ship never yaws, so that one rotation is the whole conversion, done by the input source, never by the sim. Bots and remote players set look_yaw too; pitch never enters the frame — no rule reads it. 

class_name InputFrame extends RefCounted
const SHOVE := 1          # held; tap vs hold is the sim's call (SH4)
const BRACE := 2          # held (SH4)
const RESERVED_3 := 4     # kept free for a third verb (Q2)
var seat: int
var tick: int
var move: Vector2i        # ship-plane x/z, each axis -127..127, length clamped to 127
var look_yaw: int         # 0..65535 = one turn: the facing this seat chooses (rev 3; replaces aim)
var buttons: int

Rejected: per-action command objects (ShoveCommand, BraceCommand) as in Grid Commanders. In real time "the player is holding shove" is state across ticks, not an event, and a held-buttons frame is what the network has to carry anyway. Grid Commanders' split survives whole — input → validate → apply → typed events → animate — at tick granularity.

D4 · Determinism — one seed, a fixed order, same build ⇒ same match

locked

Randomness comes from seeded RandomNumberGenerator streams only: the match stream (spawn shuffle) owned by MatchState; the sinking stream, seeded from (match seed, "sink") and drawn only while SinkSchedule is built, so the ship's pose stays a pure function of (scenario, seed, tick) whatever else rolled first; and one stream per bot seeded from (match seed, seat), so adding a bot never shifts another's rolls. Seats are processed in ascending seat id; nothing order-sensitive iterates a Dictionary; nothing reads a clock. Same seed + same input log on the same build and platform ⇒ the same digest, byte for byte, checked three ways from SH1: the match run twice in-process, replayed from its recorded log, and compared with a committed golden for the platform it was recorded on. The digest hashes the snapshot quantized (1 mm, 1 cm/s, 0.01°) every 30 ticks. Cross-platform bit identity is not promised and not needed — online play is server-authoritative (D11), so a client that drifts is corrected, never desynced.

Rejected: fixed-point integer kinematics. It buys a cross-platform guarantee the architecture does not need, at the price of fighting Vector3 on every line of GDScript.

D5 · The snapshot is the whole truth

locked

MatchSim.snapshot() returns everything the next tick depends on, and MatchSim.from_snapshot(snapshot(), config) continues bit-identically. Presentation reads snapshots only — never a live PlayerState — so the offline renderer and the future online client are the same code. This is the invariant online play stands on: a client handed a snapshot must be able to re-run the sim from it. It is gated from SH1 by a continuation test over the golden match, and every milestone that adds state extends the snapshot in the same PR. Deliberately not in the snapshot: the ship's pose and its collapsed platforms (derived from the scenario, the seed and the tick, D7), the data files (identified by a hash in MatchConfig), and bot brains (bots replay from the input log; online they live on the server).

D6 · The ship is data, in ship space; collision never comes from a mesh

locked

A ShipLayout resource lists platforms (axis-aligned rectangles at a height), ramps (rectangles whose height runs linearly along one axis), blockers (boxes and upright cylinders with a height range), railing spans along platform edges with gaps, spawn points and, from SH10, props — all in ship-local metres: x toward the bow, z to starboard, y up, origin on the main deck amidships. Every body lives in ship space, so collision is rectangles and circles, and the sea is a tilted plane in that space. The greybox meshes are generated from the layout; the art pass aligns to it and a lint checks the alignment (SH14). Nothing in the format assumes the steamer's size or deck count, and every spatial question — what is under a body, what is near it — goes through Surfaces (D13), so the spatial index a Titanic-sized hull needs (SH18) arrives behind it without changing a caller.

Rooms are the same kinds (rev 3). A wall is a thin box blocker (0.2 m) from its floor to the deck above, and a door is the gap between two walls — an opening 1.1 m wide and 2.1 m tall, with nothing that closes (Q15). A lower deck is platforms at negative heights under the main deck, which becomes rectangles that leave an opening over each stair. Stairs are steep ramps, up to about 40° — ramps, not ladders; the slope rule reads the ship's tilt, never a ramp's own pitch, so a steep stair is walkable. A ceiling is not data: the platform above is the ceiling (a roof you can stand on is already a platform over walls), and nothing in the rules looks up. A ShipRoom annotation — name, area, floor — names a room for the lint, the walk graph, the HUD and the drawing; the rules never read it. Walls stop bodies and shoves exactly as blockers do: a body slammed into a wall stops, it never vaults one.

Rejected: authoring the ship as a scene of CSG or StaticBody3D and reading its colliders — the sim would depend on the tree, and a surface the sim cannot see is one a player can stand on visually but not in the rules. A later editor tool may bake a layout from a scene; the sim still reads the .tres.

D7 · The sinking is a scenario — a pure function of (scenario, seed, tick)

locked

SinkSchedule.pose_at(tick) returns the sink depth, trim, heel and which scheduled events have fired for the match's SinkScenario — a value MatchConfig carries, chosen per match — with any jitter drawn from the sinking stream while the schedule is built (D4). Trim and heel are signed (positive trim is bow down, positive heel is starboard down) and the scenario says when the sinking starts. The MVP ships one kind of scenario — authored keyframes and events — and the steamer going down by the head is one scenario value, not a rule: nothing under core/ or ai/ may assume which end or side goes under first, or when. SH16 adds generated scenarios, where a seeded breach place, moment and pace produce the curves. The water is the world plane y = 0, and "flooded" is geometry: a point is wet when its world height is below the plane. Nothing a player does speeds up or slows the sinking, which is what lets a client compute the ship's pose itself and keeps the snapshot small. SinkSchedule replaces the scaffold's seconds-driven WaterLevel: one water authority. Inside the ship the rule is the same (rev 3): water inside a room is the sea plane — no compartments, no air pockets — so a cabin floods exactly when its floor is below the sea, and a lower deck stays dry only while it stands above it. That is why SH3b raises the steamer's freeboard.

Rejected: a buoyancy or compartment simulation (a second simulation to tune before anything is fun, and not authorable to a timeline — SH16's generated scenarios are parametric curves from a breach, not a flooding model); and "the sea speeds up when two remain", a classic shrinking-zone trick that would make the pose depend on match state.

D8 · Bodies are upright circles on surfaces; a jump is a small vertical impulse from the ground

locked

A brawler is a 0.4 m-radius circle in the deck plane with a height and a vertical state: GROUNDED on a surface, AIRBORNE, (from SH5) SWIMMING, or OUT. Height changes only by ramps, falls, a vault over a railing, a jump and the water. A jump (SH4b) is a press, never a hold, from the ground — idle, its brace let go, unstaggered, not frozen in a hit-stop, with jump_cost of stamina — that lifts the feet jump_height (0.9 m) in ship space; gravity, the world's turned by the pose, brings them down and pulls a jumper downhill as on the deck, and the air steers it only at air_control of ground acceleration. D8 once refused it for the geometry questions it raises; each has a rule. Ledge catches: there are none — a jumper lands on the highest surface under its centre as its feet come down, and a deck, a stair or a lintel overhead stops its head (Surfaces.ceiling). Landings on narrow perches: 0.9 m reaches the 0.8 m hatch and the low props and never tops a 1.0 m railing, which holds a jumper as it holds any body in the air, so a railed perch keeps its defence; a jump's landing staggers only for the drop below where it left. Same tick: a shove and a jump pressed together — the shove wins, and the seat stays on the ground. Shoves in mid-air: a shove reaches a body whose feet are within a body height of its shover's, so a jump from the same deck never escapes one, and a jumper hit in the air flies with the full knockback and no friction — brace stays the legible defence.

Rejected: capsules colliding in full 3D. A jump on a button was rejected here, as a later "feel" addition that changes every perch's defence, until your 2026-10-03 given adopted it as the rule above (SH4b).

D9 · No health, no items, one way out

locked

Your brief as a rule the code can hold: the only elimination is the sea. No damage numbers, no health, no pickups, no loadouts. Shove is the only verb in SH1–SH3; the second-verb set is Q2 (recommended: brace plus a charged shove on the same two buttons, with stamina governing brace and charge).

D10 · Bots are players

locked

A bot is a BotBrain in ai/ (Node-free, already covered by the scaffold's check) that reads a BotView — the snapshot from reaction_ticks ago — and returns an InputFrame. It cannot do anything a player cannot, and sees nothing a player could not see on screen: the current pose and water, never the schedule's future. It rolls only its own seeded stream. Difficulty is a BotProfile and nothing else. Online, bots run on the server only and reach clients as ordinary seats in snapshots. Rooms strain the promise above (rev 3): until SH7 filters BotView by line of sight through walls plus a short hearing radius (Q16), a bot sees bodies behind walls that a first-person player cannot (R16).

Rejected: bots that steer by writing velocities; bots that read SinkSchedule ahead of the current tick.

D11 · Online is server-authoritative snapshots with client re-simulation

locked

The server runs MatchRunner + MatchSim exactly as the offline game does. Clients send inputs (each packet repeats the last four frames); the server applies a seat's frame on its tick and, if it has not arrived, repeats that seat's last one; it broadcasts snapshots carrying an ack of the last input it applied per client. A client predicts by resetting to the latest snapshot and re-running MatchSim.step over its unacknowledged inputs; its own brawler is drawn from that prediction, the others interpolated about 100 ms behind. The transport sits behind a Transport interface; WebSocket first (Q8). A snapshot lists seats by id and may be a per-client subset — at the destination's seat counts the server sends each client what is near it (SH22) — so a client never assumes it holds every seat, and the verdict is the server's alone. SH1–SH10 owe this design nothing but the invariants listed in §8.

Rejected: lockstep and rollback — both need bit-identical floats across native arm64, x86-64 and WebAssembly clients, which D4 deliberately does not promise; and interpolation without prediction — a full round trip of delay on your own movement.

D12 · Feel that moves bodies is a rule; feel that moves pixels is presentation

locked

Hit-stop, recoil, stagger and landing lag change where bodies are and when, so they live in BrawlRules and the sim, and the network carries them for free. Camera shake, squash and stretch, particles, sound and the smoothing of a prediction correction change only what is drawn, and live in scenes/. A presentation effect may never hold back or speed up the sim — a visual hit-stop that paused the renderer while the sim ran on would put the picture behind the rules.

D13 · Single authorities — ask them, never re-derive

locked
  • Ticks — seconds to ticks, once per match.
  • SinkSchedule — the ship's pose, the water, which scheduled events have fired, for whichever SinkScenario the match carries. Nothing else computes a water height or decides which way the ship goes down.
  • Surfaces (SH3; a one-platform version in SH1) — what is under a body, where a falling body lands, whether a point is wet — and the only door to spatial queries, so an index can arrive behind it (SH18).
  • ShoveResolver — who a shove hits. It takes candidate bodies as data (positions, radii, states), never the live seat list, so SH13 can hand it rewound positions for lag compensation without touching the rule. Its pure read would_hit is what the first-person crosshair asks (SH9) — never a second opinion.
  • Surfaces.line_of_sight(a, b) (SH7) — whether a wall stands between two points: the one answer bots' sight and any in-sight cue on the HUD ask.
  • MatchRunner — the one loop. The scene, the headless tools, the tests and the server all step the sim through it.
  • MatchConfig — the typed statement of one match (seed, seats, rules, ship, sinking scenario, data hash). The only way a match starts, mirroring Grid Commanders' MatchRequest.

D14 · First person — every player sees through their own brawler's eyes

locked · rev 3

What "eliminated" means

  • SH1–SH4: the water is a wall. A body whose feet are below the plane is out that tick — shoved in, fallen in, or standing on a deck as it floods. Placement is the order out; seats out on the same tick share a placement.
  • From SH5 (Q1, recommended): the water is a countdown. In the sea you swim slowly with a 4 s cold meter. You can climb out onto any deck edge within 0.6 m of the surface — which, on a ship going down by the head, is the low end, exactly where the fight is. Climbing takes 0.5 s and a shove puts you back in. The meter refills slowly on deck, so a second dunking kills faster than the first. Since rev 3 the steamer rides higher (freeboard 3.4 m), so going over the side early is final, and the first waterline anyone climbs out of is inside the ship — the stairs up from a flooding lower deck.
  • Why the countdown wins. Instant death makes every edge a cliff and every match a string of one-hit outs. The countdown turns the waterline into the most contested strip on the ship (guarding the edge against climbers is the brawl's best moment), forgives one mistake, and still always ends — swimming never refills the meter.
  • Ties. Out on the same tick → shared placement. After the plunge, swimmers are ranked by cold meter left. Everyone out on the same tick → a draw, "the sea wins".

Keeping the end tense — height buys time, not safety

  1. The high ground moves. Going down by the head lifts the stern (diagram above): the bridge is the top until mid-match, the poop deck after. Any scenario that trims the ship moves the high ground the same way, toward the end that rises.
  2. Steep decks cost. Past the 14° grip angle everyone slides downhill — toward the bow in the steamer's scenario — unless they walk uphill or brace, and brace burns stamina (SH4).
  3. Lurches. Three telegraphed heel swings (a creak and a shake one second ahead) slide everyone toward one rail — worst on the unrailed roofs.
  4. The top perch collapses (Q6). The bridge roof gives way at about 2:15 with a 3 s warning: the one single-route perch is the one that cannot be held.
  5. No chokes where it matters. The layout lint gives every perch meant for the endgame at least two routes; ramps, not ladders.
  6. Bots pressure the top. king_of_hill_bias sends bots at whoever stands highest (SH7).
  7. A hard cap. By 3:30 every surface is under; the cold meter settles the rest.

Measured, not hoped: SH7's arena reports the share of matches that reach the plunge with three or more still dry. Target ≤ 10%.

Compartments: no. There are no interiors, no air pockets and no water flowing through doors. Flooding is the plane crossing geometry (D7). Interiors are a different game — corridors, doors, line of sight — and stay out of scope (§13).

The brawl

The verbs, and the triangle they make

  • Move. Analog, relative to where you look, 5 m/s, quick to start and stop. Your facing is your look (first person, D14), turned by the mouse or the right stick; bots turn at their profile's rate.
  • Shove (SH1). 3 ticks of windup, 3 active, 8 of recovery. A 40° cone in front, 0.9 m beyond the two bodies. A hit sends the target sliding at 8 m/s and staggers it for 0.4 s: no control, no grip. The windup is too short to react to — the counter to a quick shove is spacing and reads, not reflexes.
  • Charged shove (SH4, Q2). Hold shove past 0.2 s: you walk slowly and glow; release for an 11 m/s push that ignores a brace. Readable, punishable, costs stamina.
  • Brace (SH4, Q2). Hold to plant: rooted, and a shove into your front arc loses 75% of its force. It also holds you on a steep deck. Drains stamina.

Knockback rules

  • Direction is where the shover looks at the start of the active window (its facing, D14); at windup start the shove — never the view — bends toward the nearest target inside a 30° cone, the same for everyone.
  • A staggered body slides with low friction (8 m/s²): a quick shove on a flat deck moves a body about 3 m. Hitting a staggered body adds 25% (a combo).
  • Slopes add to it. Gravity in ship space pulls a sliding body downhill, so a shove downhill travels farther than one uphill — on a listing ship, which way you shove matters.
  • The railing rule (SH2). A railing stops anything crossing it slower than 7 m/s. At or above that, the body tips over it and falls. On a flat deck a quick shove sends you over only if you were pinned within about 1 m of the rail; a charged shove from about 4.5 m; downhill, farther.
  • Bodies push bodies. Circles separate; a fast body hitting another hands over part of its speed (restitution 0.2) — shoving into a crowd is a bowling ball.
  • The shover rocks back 1.5 m/s on a hit, and from SH9 both freeze for a 3-tick hit-stop — a rule, not an effect (D12).

Hazards, later — and the shape they must take

Every hazard is sim state with a rule, drawn by the scene; none is a physics body. SH6 brings the scheduled ones (lurches, the bridge collapse, a railing span that fails on a timer), because they are pure functions of the tick. Bots, camera and controls

Bots

  • Decision model: utility intents, then steering. Every think_period ticks the brain scores a handful of intents and keeps the best (with hysteresis, so it does not dither): seek high ground, line up a shove (approach so the target sits between you and the nearest water or unrailed edge — the one skill that separates good brawlers), evade or brace against a facing, winding-up opponent, recover uphill when near an edge on a slope, guard the edge against a climber (SH5), climb back when swimming. Every tick in between it steers toward the intent's goal and emits an InputFrame.
  • Perception. A BotView built from the snapshot reaction_ticks ago: every body's position, velocity, state and facing, the current pose, which surfaces are dry, the distance to the waterline. No fog on an open deck, no future schedule (D10). Rooms change this (rev 3): until SH7 adds line of sight and hearing (Q16), bots see through walls a first-person player cannot (R16).
  • Navigation. From SH3, a coarse walk graph built from the layout: platforms are nodes, ramps are edges; the brain paths to a platform, then steers locally. From SH3b the graph's zones are rooms and open-deck areas joined by doorways and stairs, so paths go through doors, never walls.
  • How many. Eight seats per match by default — you plus seven bots (Q4). The sim is O(n²) in body pairs with n ≤ 16, which is nothing; SH7 records the measured cost per tick and the budget is ≤ 2 ms for sim + bots at eight seats on the MacBook Air, with 16 seats checked for headroom.

Authority, prediction, hosting

  • Authority: a headless Godot (--headless -- --server, exported in the editor's dedicated-server mode) hosts rooms; each room is one MatchRunner at 30 Hz. Clients only ever send inputs; the server clamps them (D3) — that is the whole anti-cheat.
  • Prediction: the client runs ahead of the server by half its round trip plus a small buffer, so its frame for tick T arrives before T is stepped. It predicts the whole world with remote seats repeating their last input, but draws only its own seat from the prediction. A correction is smoothed over ~100 ms in the scene (D12).
  • Lag compensation (SH13) only if SH12's soak shows shoves that "should have hit": the server tests a shove against target positions rewound to what the shover saw, capped at 150 ms — possible because ShoveResolver takes positions as data (D13).
  • Hosting: the mini PC's established pattern — a compose stack (server container + static web build + cloudflared), .env.example, bin/deploy, a deploy job on the per-repo self-hosted runner. The web export stays single-threaded (the default), so no special COOP/COEP headers are needed.
  • Capacity: a quantized snapshot is ≈ 220 bytes at eight seats; at 30 Hz that is ≈ 6.6 kB/s per client, ≈ 0.4 Mbit/s of upload for a full room. A home line carries several rooms. These are the MVP's eight-seat sums; a Titanic-sized room needs interest management and delta snapshots (SH22).

What SH1–SH10 must do so online is never a rewrite

  • The sim is Node-free and clock-free, enforced by check from SH1 (D1, D2).
  • Every control enters as a quantized InputFrame; the sim never knows who is local (D3).
  • from_snapshot(snapshot()) continues bit-identically, tested on every PR (D5).
  • Presentation reads snapshots only and interpolates two of them (D5).
  • The ship's pose is derived from seed + tick, never stored or mutated by play (D7).
  • Bots are seats behind an InputSource, never special-cased in the sim (D10).
  • ShoveResolver takes candidate positions as data (D13).
  • MatchConfig carries a hash of the data files the match loaded, and the InputLog records it — later a refused handshake, today a replay that refuses to run against changed numbers.

Risks

R1 · high — The brawl does not feel good

Hand-rolled kinematics can feel like ice or like mud, and no test catches it. Mitigation: every feel number is data (§6), so tuning is a .tres edit; SH1 is playable on day one, so feel is judged early and every milestone; SH9 is a milestone devoted to it; hit-stop and recoil are rules, so they survive the network. Each milestone's PR body carries one sentence on how it played from whoever ran make run.

R2 · high — A field the snapshot forgot

Add state to PlayerState but not to the snapshot and offline play still works — and online prediction silently diverges months later. Mitigation: the continuation test from SH1 (D5), run over the golden match and over a scripted scenario for each mechanic as it lands (stamina in SH4, the cold meter in SH5, hit-stop in SH9, crates in SH10), because a field the golden match never exercises escapes a test that only replays the golden match.

R3 · high — The endgame stalls into camping

The genre's oldest failure: the last three hold the best spot and wait. Mitigation: §5's seven mechanisms, led by the physics of a ship sinking by the head moving the high ground; SH7's arena measures it (≤ 10% of matches reach the plunge with three or more dry); every lever is data — event times, the grip angle, the collapse.

R4 · high — Agents cannot playtest

The most important property of a real-time game is the one an agent cannot see. Mitigation: every rule is in core/ under GUT; SH1's instruments (make match, make capture, --autoplay) give headless or one-shot evidence; SH7 turns "is the endgame the right shape" into numbers; the make run gates are explicitly for you or the QA agent, and the review reads the PR's play note.

R5 · high — Genre gravity

Battle royales attract loot, skins, progression and ranked play. Mitigation: D9 and §13 fence it; anything on that list is a new plan, never a milestone's extra.

R6 · medium — Float drift between platforms

Vector3 is single precision and the engine's maths is compiled per platform (fused multiply-add, the C library's sin/cos), so a match recorded on the arm64 Mac may not reproduce bit for bit on CI's x86-64 Linux, and one differing bit can grow through a collision branch. Mitigation: D4 promises same build, same platform only; goldens are per platform and a missing one is pending, not failing; the twice-run, log-replay and continuation tests run everywhere and catch the real defects (an unseeded roll, hidden state, an order dependence). Online play never needed cross-platform identity (D11).

R7 · medium — GDScript is too slow for sim + bots + prediction

Eight bodies at 30 Hz are cheap; bots' scoring and SH11's re-simulation of several ticks per snapshot multiply it. Budget: sim + bots ≤ 2 ms per tick at eight seats, measured in SH7 and again in SH11. Fallbacks, in order: a slower think rate for easy and normal bots; caching per-tick geometry queries; early-outs on distant pairs; last, a GDExtension for the hot loop — a plan decision with a toolchain cost, never a milestone's quiet fix.

R8 · medium — WebSocket's head-of-line stalls

Over TCP, one lost segment holds up everything behind it. Mitigation: redundant inputs and full snapshots mean a stall costs latency, never correctness; the Transport interface keeps the swap cheap; SH12 measures it; WebRTC data channels (browsers) and ENet (native) are Q8's fallbacks.

R9 · medium — A game server at home

Upload, uptime, a home machine on the internet. Mitigation: Cloudflare Tunnel (no open ports), room codes, clamped inputs, no accounts, a per-address join limit; §8's arithmetic says a room is about 0.4 Mbit/s of upload. If it outgrows the mini PC, the same container moves to a rented server unchanged.

R10 · medium — First person in greybox does not read

What is beside or behind you, how far a drop is, which way is downhill, where the water is when you are inside. Mitigation (rev 3): the inclinometer and the height-above-the-sea readout (SH9), off-screen windup arcs (SH9) and positional footsteps (SH15), seat colour, hat and an in-sight chevron on every brawler, a level horizon the tilt reads against (Q17), and blob shadows under others. Nothing is drawn through walls — the silhouette idea is retired with the elevated camera.

R11 · low — The editor helper autoload in exported builds

The scaffold registers _mcp_game_helper (godot_ai) as an autoload. Grid Commanders' mobile plan measured on Godot 4.7.1 that a feature-tagged override does not clear an autoload, and that excluding the addon while an entry still names it is a boot error. Mitigation: SH12 settles it once, for the server and the web export: the plugin registers its runtime singleton itself (Grid Commanders' recorded fallback), or the helper ships — measured, not assumed.

R12 · low — Two water authorities

The scaffold's WaterLevel would be a second answer to "how high is the water", in seconds rather than ticks. SH1 deletes it with its test (D7, D13).

R13 · high — The destination eats the MVP

A Titanic-sized goal pulls work forward before the small game is fun. Mitigation: SH16–SH23 start only after SH15; the MVP's changes for the destination are wording and tests only (the audit in §3); no MVP gate waits on a destination system.

R14 · medium — A huge ship is mostly empty deck

Scale can dilute the brawl into a walk. Mitigation: seats (Q12), pace (Q13) and spawn spread are data; SH20's arena reports idle time and meetings per minute on the big ship before anything is tuned.

R15 · high — Motion sickness on a listing ship

First person on a deck that tilts for minutes, plus lurches and view kicks, is the textbook trigger. Mitigation: a level horizon by default (Q17), no head-bob, a field-of-view slider (75–110°), shakes and kicks short and small (SH9b), the deck-roll slider at 0 by default; every milestone's play note says whether anyone felt it.

R16 · high — Omniscient bots against a blind player

Bots read the whole delayed snapshot — through walls and behind you — while a first-person player sees about 90°. Mitigation: perception already lags; SH7 filters BotView by line of sight plus 6 m hearing (Q16); the arena reports the win-share gap between sighted and omniscient bots, so the price of fairness is a number.

R17 · medium — Authoring rooms by hand

Walls, doors and stairs typed as rectangles into a .tres are slow and easy to get wrong. Mitigation: SH3b's lint (doors, no sealed room, two stairs up, nothing crossing a stair) catches the wrong; the steamer has about a dozen rooms, which is hand-sized. Rule: if SH3b's PR reports hand-authoring as its bottleneck, SH17's bake tool moves into the MVP before more rooms are added — decided then, not pre-paid now.

R18 · low — Performance inside rooms

Fine, and said so: walls add roughly fifty box blockers, so collision stays a few hundred cheap checks per tick at eight seats, and a lit interior with a lamp per room is light work for gl_compatibility on the Air. SH14b measures frame time on the flooding lower deck so "fine" is a number.

R19 · medium — A shove's windup is hard to read in first person

You only see what is in front of you, and a quick shove's windup is 100 ms. Mitigation: the windup pose reads from the front and the side (SH14); off-screen arcs (SH9) and a positional grunt and whoosh (SH15) cover the rest; the crosshair says when your own shove would land. The quick shove was always meant to be beaten by spacing and reads; the charged shove's glow and sound are the tells that matter.
