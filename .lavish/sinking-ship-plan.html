Sinking Ship — last one dry wins

A battle royale on a ship that is going down. Eight brawlers on a small coastal steamer, no loot and no weapons: you shove, you brace, you push people into the sea. The rising water and the listing deck are the shrinking zone. Today the steamer sinks by the head on a script, so the bow goes under first, the stern climbs, the decks tilt until standing still is work, and the last one standing on dry planks wins. You play it in first person, through your own brawler's eyes, and the steamer has an inside — a hold, an engine room, cabins and a saloon — that floods first and drives everyone upward. Rev 4 (2026-10-05, for your review) replaces the script with physics: each match a seeded iceberg strikes somewhere different, each compartment floods on its own, trapped air is squeezed and leaks, and the ship trims, lists, capsizes or breaks only when the physics says so — on several ships, ending with the Titanic (§5b).

The one thing this plan cannot get wrong is the shape of the simulation, because it is the one thing that cannot be retrofitted: bots now, online later, without a rewrite. The answer (§2) is a Node-free, 30 Hz simulation in core/ that Godot only draws — the same input → validate → apply → typed events → animate split Grid Commanders keeps, at tick granularity. Fifteen milestones make the MVP — first playable at SH1, a full single-player game at SH10, online at SH12, art last — and you approved them as the MVP on 2026-10-02. Eight more (SH16–SH23) lead to the destination you named: a ship as big as the Titanic that can go down by any side at any time. Rev 4 replaces SH16 and SH19 with your eleven steps, SH24–SH34, from compartments in the ship data to the Titanic.

The game in one page

The loop

  1. Eight brawlers spawn around the main deck. A three-second countdown, then the horn.
  2. Everyone shoves everyone. A shove is a short, committed push; a body knocked into the sea is out (§5 for exactly when).
  3. The ship goes down. Today she goes by the head on SH6's script: the bow floods, the deck lists, the stern rises. From rev 4 (§5b) she goes however a seeded iceberg hit makes her go — by the head or the stern, onto one side, over or in pieces, but always down (your answer to Q18) — and every few seconds the dry area is smaller and steeper.
  4. Late on, standing still on a tilted deck is work — walk uphill or brace, or slide toward the water. Lurches throw everyone toward one rail; today the highest perch gives way on a timer, from rev 4 only when something physical brings it down.
  5. The last one dry wins. Results, rematch, another seed.

What a three-minute match feels like

  • 0:00–0:45 · Calm. Fights on every level, from the hold to the bridge; the ship barely moves.
  • 0:45–1:30 · The flooding below. The sea pours into the hold at the bow and chases everyone aft and up the stairs; the companionways are the fight.
  • 1:30–2:40 · By the head, then the list. The forward main deck and the forecastle go under, the saloon floods, the deck lists one way then the other; the wheelhouse roof collapses.
  • 2:40–3:30 · The plunge. The stern stands up out of the sea, the decks are steeper than anyone can stand on for free, and the last two or three fight on the poop deck while it slides under.

This is today's scripted steamer. From SH26 (rev 4) the phases come from the hit. She always sinks (Q18), and for now one physics second is one match second (Q20, deferred), so a match lasts until the brawl ends it or she is gone (§5b).

What the player does

  • Look (rev 3) — first person: the mouse or the right stick turns you, and you shove where you look.
  • Move — analog, relative to where you look; Space jumps (SH4b).
  • Shove — tap: quick and weak; hold (from SH4): a charged shove that breaks a brace.
  • Brace (from SH4) — plant your feet: shrugs off a shove from the front and holds you on a steep deck, at a stamina cost.
  • Nothing else. No health, no items, no pickups. The water is the only way out.

The central decision — who owns where a body is

A real-time 3D brawl is not a turn-based grid, so Grid Commanders' architecture does not transfer for free. The question is who answers "where is every body, and what touched what, this tick". There are two serious answers. (a) A custom Node-free fixed-tick kinematic simulation in core/: characters as circles on deck surfaces, shoves as impulses, the ship as a handful of tilting platforms, the water as a plane, and Godot nodes drawing interpolated state. (b) Godot physics — CharacterBody3D on Jolt, which became the default 3D physics engine for new projects in Godot 4.6 — running on a headless authoritative server later.

Criterion(a) Custom kinematic sim in core/(b) Godot physics (CharacterBody3D, Jolt)
Tested under GUT without a scene tree Yes. MatchSim.step(frames) is a function call: a test builds a state, steps thirty ticks, asserts. Thousands of ticks a second, headless, in the scaffold's existing make test. No. Bodies need a SceneTree and a physics space; a test awaits real physics frames, and collision results depend on the engine's step. It also fights the scaffold's own rule that core/ holds no Node.
Determinism Ours to keep: seeded streams, a fixed processing order, no clock. Same build + same seed + same inputs ⇒ the same match, checked against a golden digest from SH1. Jolt is built to be deterministic for identical inputs in identical order, but the engine owns when and in what order bodies step. Nothing gives a test or a server a seed-and-replay contract over a whole scene.
Becoming server-authoritative Direct. The server runs the same MatchRunner as the offline game; it needs no scene at all, so a headless room is a few hundred kilobytes of state. Feasible: a headless Godot runs the full scene, and MultiplayerSynchronizer replicates transforms.
Client prediction Reset to the last snapshot and re-run MatchSim.step over the inputs the server has not acknowledged — cheap at eight bodies, and the same code as the server's. The engine offers no snapshot/restore of a physics world. Prediction means replaying move_and_slide for your own body against others that cannot be rewound — or no prediction, i.e. a full round trip of delay on your own movement in a game about timing.
Brawl feel Every number is ours: acceleration, friction, knockback, slide, the railing rule. The cost is that ragdolls, tumbling crates and stacking debris cannot matter to the rules; they become presentation, or our own simple bodies (SH10's crates). Tumbling, ragdolls and stacking for free — and the classic problem that comes with them: physics chaos is hard to tune, hard to read on screen and hard to network.
Build effort, part-time, through agents Roughly 1–2k lines of plain geometry by SH3, all test-first — the kind of work an agent can verify on its own, because GUT is the evidence. A faster first evening, then slower every week after: most behaviour can only be verified by a human playing, and the online milestone turns into a rewrite.
Level authoring The ship is a .tres of rectangles, ramps and upright cylinders (D6); the greybox is generated from it and the art must align to it. Paint colliders in the editor; any mesh can be ground.
Web export Fine, and the server never renders. Fine on the client; the server carries the whole scene.
Verdict Recommended. Rejected for gameplay; allowed for presentation-only effects.

Recommendation: (a). Every rule of the match — movement, slopes, railings, shoves, water, eliminations, the verdict — lives in RefCounted classes under core/ that advance in fixed 30 Hz ticks from one InputFrame per seat. Godot nodes only read snapshots and interpolate between the last two, so the offline game, the headless tools, the tests and a future server all run the exact same code. It is the only option where "becomes server-authoritative with client prediction" is a transport problem rather than a rewrite, and where an agent can prove a behaviour without a human at the keyboard.

What (a) rules out

  • Physics-driven bodies in gameplay — ragdolls, tumbling crates, debris that the rules read. A body the sim does not own cannot be predicted, replayed or made authoritative.
  • Collision from meshes or editor-painted colliders, and arbitrary 3D terrain. The ship is axis-aligned rectangles, ramps and upright cylinders in ship space (D6); a curved hull is drawn, not collided.
  • Wall-climbing and full 3D capsule contact (D8).
  • Jolt or GodotPhysics3D anywhere on the server.
  • Lockstep and rollback netcode (D11). Not ruled out by (a) itself — (a) makes them possible — but by D4's choice not to chase cross-platform bit-identical floats.

What (a) keeps

  • Godot physics for pixels. A ragdoll for an eliminated brawler, a splash, falling sparks, a flapping flag — anything the sim never reads back. That rule is D12.
  • The Grid Commanders split, intact. Input → validate → apply → typed events → animate; the AI issues the same input as a player; seeded RNG; a golden replay; GUT over the Node-free layers.
  • The editor. Scenes, the HUD, the camera, materials and the art pass are ordinary Godot. Only the rules moved out.
  • Web export. gl_compatibility is the one renderer the web platform supports, and nothing in the sim depends on threads.

Sources: Godot 4.6 release notes — Jolt is the default physics engine for new 3D projects, existing projects unaffected (godotengine.org/releases/4.6). Godot 4.7 docs, Exporting for the Web — web exports support only the Compatibility renderer; low-level networking is not implemented; only HTTP, WebSocket (client) and WebRTC are available; single-threaded export is the default (docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/…/exporting_for_web).

The scaffold's tools/check_scripts.sh already refuses a Node, a scene, the tree and the global RNG under core/ and ai/; SH1 adds the engine's clocks and devices to the same refusal — Time., OS., Engine., Input., DisplayServer. — so the rule is enforced rather than remembered.

D2 · 30 Hz, and the tick is the only clock

locked · reworded rev 4

The sim advances in fixed steps of 1/30 s. Nothing in the match sim or ai/ takes an engine delta or reads a time; a duration is a count of ticks. The sinking physics (rev 4) is the one integrator with a step of its own: it advances simulated seconds by a step it chooses from the ship's state (0.05–10 s, est.), deterministically, inside the bake before the match uses it — never from a frame or a clock — and its timeline reaches the match through one conversion. Ticks.RATE = 30 is a code constant, not a tuning value: changing it moves every timing, the wire format and the golden. Data is authored in seconds and metres for humans and converted once per match by Ticks.from_seconds (round half away from zero), the single conversion authority — rev 4's timeline included, through the clock mapping its scenario names, one to one for now (Q20, deferred). Why 30 and not 60: half the GDScript cost for the sim, the bots and the client's re-simulation, half the snapshot bandwidth out of a home connection, and a shove's windup (3 ticks, 100 ms) still reads. Rendering runs at the display rate by interpolating the last two ticks, so 30 Hz never shows as stutter.

D3 · One door in: an InputFrame per seat per tick

locked · extended rev 4

Every seat — the local human, a bot, a remote player, a replay — produces exactly one InputFrame per tick, and MatchSim.step(frames) is the only way anything happens. The frame is quantized when it is created (move as integers −127…127 per axis, the look yaw as 0…65 535 for one turn), so an offline match and the same match over a wire see bit-identical inputs. Buttons are held state; presses and releases are derived inside the sim from the previous tick's buttons, so a lost packet cannot eat a press once redundancy restores the frame. The sim validates every frame — clamps it, ignores buttons from an eliminated or staggered seat — because a frame is a request, never a fact. The sim never learns which seats are human, where the camera is, or what device sent a frame. Move is expressed in the ship's horizontal plane. Since rev 3 the game is first person (D14): the frame also carries the seat's look_yaw — the facing it chooses this tick, quantized to 1/65 536 of a turn — and InputFrame.from_screen(stick, look_yaw) turns the stick into a move relative to that look (stick up walks where you look), computed against the quantized yaw so both ends of a wire agree. The ship never yaws, so that one rotation is the whole conversion, done by the input source, never by the sim. Bots and remote players set look_yaw too; pitch never enters the frame — no rule reads it.

D6 · The ship is data, in ship space; collision never comes from a mesh

locked · reworded rev 4

A ShipLayout resource lists platforms (axis-aligned rectangles at a height), ramps (rectangles whose height runs linearly along one axis), blockers (boxes and upright cylinders with a height range), railing spans along platform edges with gaps, spawn points and, from SH10, props — all in ship-local metres: x toward the bow, z to starboard, y up, origin on the main deck amidships. Every body lives in ship space — from SH33, the space of the piece of hull it is on — so collision is rectangles and circles, and the sea outside is a tilted plane in that space; the water inside is each cell's own level (rev 4, D7). What "eliminated" means

  • SH1–SH4: the water is a wall. A body whose feet are below the plane is out that tick — shoved in, fallen in, or standing on a deck as it floods. Placement is the order out; seats out on the same tick share a placement.
  • From SH5 (Q1, recommended): the water is a countdown. In the sea you swim slowly with a 4 s cold meter. You can climb out onto any deck edge within 0.6 m of the surface — which, on a ship going down by the head, is the low end, exactly where the fight is. Climbing takes 0.5 s and a shove puts you back in. The meter refills slowly on deck, so a second dunking kills faster than the first. Since rev 3 the steamer rides higher (freeboard 3.4 m), so going over the side early is final, and the first waterline anyone climbs out of is inside the ship — the stairs up from a flooding lower deck.
  • Why the countdown wins. Instant death makes every edge a cliff and every match a string of one-hit outs. The countdown turns the waterline into the most contested strip on the ship (guarding the edge against climbers is the brawl's best moment), forgives one mistake, and still always ends — swimming never refills the meter.
  • Ties. Out on the same tick → shared placement. After the plunge, swimmers are ranked by cold meter left. Everyone out on the same tick → a draw, "the sea wins".

Keeping the end tense — height buys time, not safety

  1. The high ground moves. Going down by the head lifts the stern (diagram above): the bridge is the top until mid-match, the poop deck after. Any scenario that trims the ship moves the high ground the same way, toward the end that rises.
  2. Steep decks cost. Past the 14° grip angle everyone slides downhill — toward the bow in the steamer's scenario — unless they walk uphill or brace, and brace burns stamina (SH4).
  3. Lurches. Three telegraphed heel swings (a creak and a shake one second ahead) slide everyone toward one rail — worst on the unrailed roofs.
  4. The top perch collapses (Q6). The bridge roof gives way at about 2:15 with a 3 s warning: the one single-route perch is the one that cannot be held.
  5. No chokes where it matters. The layout lint gives every perch meant for the endgame at least two routes; ramps, not ladders.
  6. Bots pressure the top. king_of_hill_bias sends bots at whoever stands highest (SH7).
  7. A hard cap. By 3:30 every surface is under; the cold meter settles the rest.

5b.1 · The model in plain words

One model for every ship; each ship's data (5b.2) says what is different. It is one stepper in core/sinking/ — plain classes under the same Node-free and RNG-free check as the rest of core/, no clock, state in and state out — so the bake, the tests and the tools run the same code (D1, D7). It follows the methods of the naval-architecture flooding codes (Ruponen 2007–2009; Braidotti & Mauro 2020) and of Floating Sandbox's water and air, reimplemented, never copied (R31).

The hit

At a moment drawn from the seed, the iceberg scrapes one side. The seed decides which side, where along the hull the gash starts and how long it runs, how far under the waterline it is (it may start just above it), how wide it would be if it were one even slit, and how deep it bites. The gash becomes a set of thin openings to the sea, one for each cell it crosses, each a little bigger or smaller than its share because real damage is uneven (×0.5–1.5, research). A gash that bites less deep than a side void is wide opens only the void. The same draw then decides, once, which watertight doors jam open and which portholes were left open — each ship's data gives the chances — and weakens any watertight wall the gash ends next to. In a match, a draw that would leave her afloat is replaced by the next one (below). After that nothing is random: the rest is physics.

Cells and their water

The inside of the ship is cut into cells: every compartment, every enclosed room above, and every space nobody walks in. Each room lies in exactly one cell, and everything enclosed lies in some cell, so the ship never floats on air water cannot reach. A cell is a box with a permeability; a box that pokes outside the curved hull carries a shape factor so its volume matches. Each cell holds its own water and its own air. Its water surface is flat and level with the world wherever the ship points, so in a listing ship the water in each cell piles into that cell's low corner — and the level for a given amount of water comes from an exact formula for a tilted box, checked in the research to 10⁻¹².

Openings and flow

Water moves only through openings. Each is a rectangle in ship space, and each step its corners are turned into world heights, so it does not matter whether the ship is level, listing or upside down. Through an opening that is under water on both sides: flow = 0.6 × area × √(2 g × difference in head). In words: the flow grows with the opening's area and with the square root of how much higher the water stands on one side than on the other; 0.6 is the share of the ideal flow a real opening passes (FLOODSTAND's standard value for doors, hatches and breaches). Where water only spills over a bottom edge — a sill, the top of a low wall — the same law summed over the wet part grows with the depth over the edge to the power 1.5. A stairwell or hatch is a hole in a floor: water above it falls through at the rate the depth above it drives, and the cell below pushes back only once its own water, or its squeezed air, reaches the hole. Every transfer in a step is capped at what would level the two sides, so cells never slosh back and forth. A cell filled to its ceiling has no surface of its own; it is given a thin imaginary one above its ceiling (est. 1% of its floor, the research's device), so the push of the water behind it passes straight through a chain of full cells.

Air

A cell's air escapes freely while any opening gives it a dry path to the sky. When every path is under water, the air is trapped. Trapped air is squeezed as water rises: pressure × volume stays the same (Boyle's law, at a constant temperature), so air 10 m down takes about half its surface volume. Squeezed air pushes back on the water — a closed tank 10 m down fills only 2.18 m of its 5 m (research's worked example). Every cell also loses air slowly through rivets, seams and vents (a leak area per cell, est. by construction), so a pocket shrinks over minutes to hours. Because "trapped" is decided from world heights, a hull upside down traps air against its old floors with no special case. Trapped air in cells that touch through a dry opening — two rooms of an upturned hull joined by a doorway — is one pocket, with one pressure. This is the simple form the research recommends for a step of seconds: vented or trapped, plus one slow leak, rather than tracking air flowing cell to cell.

Floating and tipping

The hull is described by 20–40 sections: the outline of everything enclosed — hull and deckhouses — cut across at points along its length. Each step the sea's plane cuts every section; the area under water times the section's length, added up, is the volume the hull pushes aside, and that weight of sea is the lift. The water inside is added weight, at the centre of each cell's water — the method damage-stability codes use. When water runs to the low side of a wide cell its weight goes with it: that is the free-surface effect, why a little loose water in a wide space is dangerous. Lift acts through the centre of the hull's underwater part, weight through the centre of mass; while the first moves further toward the low side than the second, she rights herself. When water makes her top-heavy or lopsided faster than it makes her heavy, she capsizes; when she runs out of lift before she runs out of stability, she sinks upright. Each step solves her sinkage, trim and list together (heave, pitch and roll; she never yaws or drifts), and the attitude is carried as a rotation turned a little at a time, so she can stand on end or lie upside down.

Bending and breaking

Along the hull, weight and lift do not match station by station: a flooded bow is heavy at one end while lift holds up the stern. Adding up the mismatch from one end gives the shear; adding up the shear gives the bending moment — the force folding the hull. Each station has a strength (data). The model compares the two every step; their ratio is all that decides a break. Past 0.8 (est.) the hull creaks (an event); at 1 a hinge starts — that station loses most of its strength over 10–60 s (est., after the Marine Forensics Panel's hinge phase) — and the pieces separate. Small hulls are far stronger than their loads: a 30 m trawler holds about 66 MN·m against a few MN·m of flooding load (research est.), so she never breaks. The Titanic held about 4.6 GN·m (±15%) and was overloaded at 10–17° of trim (research; the angle is debated).

Things that give way

Every closed door, hatch, window, porthole and watertight wall has a head of water at which it starts to leak and one at which it collapses — tested values where they exist (FLOODSTAND and IMO: a cabin wall at 1.2–1.4 m, a fire door near 2 m, a semi-watertight sliding door at 8.4 m; est. for the rest). A hinged door opens readily with the flow and holds against it. A collapse is one-way. The funnel falls when the tilt passes what its stays hold (est. list 25–30°, trim 15°), when water reaches its base as she goes down, or when a hinge forms beside it; it falls toward the low side. The lights stay on while the generator's cell is dry enough and the tilt is within what machinery tolerates (SOLAS: 15° of list standing, 22.5° rolling, 7.5° of trim; thresholds est.); then emergency power, if the ship has it, runs its minutes, and a break cuts the cables. Lifeboats on the high side are useless past a list (15° on old davits, 20° under modern rules). Pumps run while there is power and can beat a small hole.

The seabed and the end

In shallow water the lowest points of the hull — keel, bilges, deck edges, funnel and mast tops — touch the bottom and are pushed back by it, with friction. Contact takes weight off the hull's lift, which makes a grounded ship less stable: that is why ships roll onto their sides in shallow water and stop there (Herald of Free Enterprise at about 95°, Costa Concordia at 65–80° on a ledge). A long ship in water shallower than her length hits bottom with her bow while the stern is still up (Britannic, Lusitania). In the physics each piece ends in one of four states: gone (wholly under water and still going down, or wholly under on the bottom), aground (on the bottom with part of her still dry), afloat (the water has stopped coming in) or afloat upside down (on air that is still leaking). The bake stops when every piece is gone, or at a cap of simulated time (5 h 30 min, est.).

How a match ends. By the brawl, as today: one seat left dry. If the brawl outlasts her, she is gone and everyone swims: the cold settles it, ranked by cold meter; anyone still inside a piece when it goes is out that tick with her, ranked the same way. There is no cap on the match. On a wreck that keeps dry footing for good — on her side on the bottom of a coast scenario — the match goes on until one player is left, with no limit (your answer to Q21). The plan reads the same into a hull floating upside down, until she goes under, and into a wreck that settles upright on the bottom with her deckhouse dry.

Every match's hit sinks her — the must-sink rule your answer to Q18

You answered “the ship can't stay afloat”. The physics still can leave a light hit afloat; a match never plays one. Before the match starts, its hit is chosen like this, all from the sinking stream in a fixed order:

  1. Draw a hit — the gash, then the doors that jam and the portholes left open (5b.1, The hit).
  2. A quick check without the bake. SH24's hydrostatics floats her with every cell the gash opens flooded to the sea, then adds each cell that would overflow from those, in turn, until nothing more overflows. If she still floats upright with dry deck to spare and her stability (GM) still positive, the hit is drawn again. This costs microseconds and throws out most light hits. It must only throw out a hit the bake would also leave afloat, never one that would sink her in some other way; the raw census checks that on every seed.
  3. Bake the hit that passes. If the bake ends with any piece still afloat — upright or upside down — when it reaches its cap, the hit is drawn again. An upside-down hull counts as gone only once her trapped air has leaked and she has gone under; her cells' leak areas decide how long that takes (research: a pocket leaking 10⁻³ m² per 1 000 m³ lasts about 2 h, one leaking 10⁻⁴ about 20 h, which this step would draw again).
  4. Bounded. At most 8 hits thrown out by the quick check and 3 bakes (est.).
  5. Then a fixed fallback, with no more drawing: the last hit is made heavier, rung by rung — each rung doubles its equivalent width and runs the gash on into the next cell, at most 6 rungs (est.), since a wider hole into one compartment alone may never sink her — until the quick check says she founders, and is baked once. If no rung does it, or that bake still leaves her afloat, the ship's sure_hit is used, with every watertight door and porthole shut: a hit in her data that make ship-check proves sinks her within the cap, in each of her scenarios. At most five bakes in all, est. 1.0–1.7 on average.

This chooses where she is struck and never touches what happens after: the accepted hit is a real hit, and the physics alone decides how she goes. Every draw comes before the accepted bake, so D4 holds and the sinking stays a pure function of (ship, scenario, seed). Explicit hits bypass it — the wreck cases, the "everything shut" checks, a HIT= given to a tool — so the physics can still report "stays afloat", and OutcomeClassifier keeps that label. The rule starts at SH26, the first milestone where the physics decides, and its tests and the match census rerun at SH27, SH29, SH31, SH32 and SH33, because each stage can change how a sinking ends. A coast scenario's wreck that comes to rest aground with part of her dry is not "afloat": it is played, and the match goes on until one player is left (Q21).

How long the step is, and why it matters

The physics chooses its own step, between 0.05 s and 10 s (est.): long while the sinking is calm, short when the ship is losing stability, an opening is about to give way, or a cell is about to reach a sill or its ceiling — the step is cut to land exactly on that moment. A fixed 1 s step for five hours would cost about 6 s of GDScript (research, timed on this M1 Air); the adaptive step costs far less — what a bake costs per ship is in 5b.4. Water transfers are capped at the amount that would level two cells, swept in a fixed order that reverses every other step — the research showed an uncapped step leaves two tanks sloshing ±0.36 m forever at a 30 s step. The attitude step is implicit: it solves where the ship is heading, not just where she is pushed, so a long step settles her instead of overshooting, and it carries on from where she was, so a capsize keeps its history.

How long a sinking takes — the rough rule

Research's rule of thumb: time ≈ the water the ship can take before she goes ÷ (0.5 × 0.6 × hole area × √(2 g × depth of the hole)). In words: double the hole and she sinks in half the time; a hole four times deeper lets water in twice as fast; 0.6 is the share of the ideal flow a real hole passes, and 0.5 says the inflow slows as the water inside rises. On the steamer that is about 620 s ÷ area in m² (est.): 2 m² sinks her in about 5 minutes and 0.03 m² in about 5 hours — if the gash opens enough compartments; a hole into one compartment alone does not sink her at any size (true of the physics; a match draws such a hit again, below). The rule only sets the hit's bands; the model decides.

"5 minutes to 5 hours" is the fleet's range, not every ship's (rule-of-thumb times from the bands each ship gets — the steamer's in 5b.2, the trawler's at SH30, the Titanic's at SH34; all est.): the trawler about 3 minutes to 5 hours (about 133 s ÷ area: 0.007–0.7 m²); the steamer about 5 minutes to 5 hours (0.03–2.4 m², the top of her bands); the Titanic about 40 minutes to 5 hours (about 10 600 s ÷ area: 0.6–4.5 m²; 2 h 40 min on the night's 1.1 m²). The Titanic cannot go in 5 minutes: by this rule that needs about 35 m² of hole, far beyond any scrape. Capsizes beat the rule on every ship (Herald went over in 90 s).

5b.3 · A ship on her side, upside down, or in pieces

Up is the world's up

Every face of the ship's boxes is a surface: the tops and undersides of decks, both sides of every wall, the outside of the hull. Which faces are floors is decided every tick from the ship's attitude: a face is a floor when it is within the stand limit of level (35°, est.). A deck stays a floor until it tilts past 35°; the wall beside it becomes a floor at 55° of roll. Between the two, nobody walks: a body that braces holds where it is, up to 55° (est., a data value, D8), so a long heavy list stays playable; anyone else slides into the corner where deck meets wall and rests there, free to move along the corner, not away from it. Past 90° the old walls are floors; at 180° the undersides of the decks are. A doorway in a wall that has become a floor is a hole you can fall through into the room beyond; a stair opening in a deck that has become a wall is a doorway. Because every face in the data is aligned with a ship axis (D6), "up in the ship" is the ship axis nearest the world's up, and every spatial search runs along it. Stairs count as floors only while the ship's own up is that axis. The hull's outside is a floor too: on her beam ends, the high side of the hull is where survivors stand.

Movement and the camera

A body stays an upright circle (D8) — upright to the world, on whatever face is under it. Gravity is the world's, turned into ship space as today, so the slope, grip and brace rules work unchanged on any face. A jump goes up the world's up. A swimmer swims in its cell's water; in a cell with no air above the water it is under, and it can pass a flooded doorway into the next cell and surface in that cell's pocket (SH29). The cold meter runs in any water, except that while a swimmer's head is in a trapped pocket it drains at a slower pocket rate (est. a quarter of the open-water rate, so today's 4 s meter lasts 16 s there), and a swimmer can climb out onto any dry face inside a pocket, as onto a deck edge today — Q22, built as recommended unless you overrule it. Whether the meter runs faster under water is a data knob (est. no). The camera keeps the world's up and the ship turns around you (D14): the eye 1.6 m above the feet along the world's up, the horizon level (Q17). Move and look are expressed in the plane of the face you stand on (D3); the input source maps them as that face changes.

Pieces

After a break each piece has its own pose in the timeline, its own cells, sections, mass and bending check. Every platform, wall, railing, ladder, room and lamp belongs to one piece; anything straddling the cut is split there, and a cell cut in two shares its water by volume. A body belongs to the piece under it — a field in the snapshot (D5) — and lives in that piece's ship space (D6); a body that jumps or falls onto another piece is carried across through world space. The torn end of each piece is a huge opening to the sea. Lights on a piece cut off from its generator go out. The hull art is pre-cut at the stations where the ship can break, so each piece is drawn on its own (R27).

Every milestone playable — the interim rules

  • SH24–SH25: the physics does not drive yet; today's script plays, and SH25's hit is seen, heard and printed.
  • SH26: no trim or list yet — she settles level as she fills.
  • SH26–SH28: no air yet — every cell vents, water rises to the sea's level.
  • SH27–SH31: past the supported attitude (45° from upright, est.) movement cannot follow her, so the match settles on that tick the way today's cap does: everyone left goes out together, the warmest first. The physics carries on; the match does not.
  • SH31–SH32: bending is computed and reported, and cannot break the hull.
  • Until SH32: no seabed — every scenario is deep water.

Each stage is a switch in SeaPhysics.stages, turned on by its milestone. The switches keep earlier tests meaningful and let a regression be found by turning stages off; no ship turns one off to steer an outcome.

5b.4 · Every outcome from the model, and the test that proves it

Outcomes are labels read off a finished timeline by OutcomeClassifier; one sinking can carry several, and no label drives anything. Each is proven in up to three layers: 1 · textbook checks with closed-form answers; 2 · real wrecks given as explicit hits, with tolerance bands as wide as the sources disagree (a wreck that is not one of our ships is a test-only hull, generated from a few published dimensions, whose assumed values are written beside it); 3 · our ships: one pinned seed per outcome per ship, plus a census over many seeds against target bands. A pinned seed names a match hit, after the must-sink rule (5b.1); a case that must end afloat is an explicit hit. Every outcome test runs the bake alone — seconds, no 30 Hz match — because a five-hour match would cost about 20 minutes headless and 4.3 million input frames; no gate anywhere plays a match for hours.

The timeline — what it holds, how big, how it travels

  • Holds: a keyframe about every simulated second — each piece's attitude and place, each cell's water and air — plus every event at its exact time with its warning time. SinkSchedule interpolates between keyframes behind its present API (pose_at, fired, events_at, phase_at; cap_tick becomes the timeline's end).
  • Compacted: a keyframe its neighbours reproduce within 1 cm of water and 0.05° of attitude (est.) is dropped, because a slow sinking is smooth; values are stored as integers (millimetres, litres), each as a difference from the last, then compressed.
  • Size: raw, one keyframe a second for five hours of the steamer is about 2.8 MB; compacted, the budget is 200 kB for the steamer's longest and 2 MB for the Titanic (est., measured in SH28 and SH34).
  • Online: the host bakes; the match-start message carries the timeline's hash; the timeline streams to clients ahead of the tick, its first minutes before the countdown ends; no client ever bakes (D4, D11). Offline it is baked on the machine that plays.
  • Bake time (stated here once): the research timed its kernels on a model the size of the steamer — about 20 partly filled cells, 80 openings and 30 stations — at 0.7–1.6 s for a 5-minute sinking and 1–2.4 s for a 5-hour one on the M1 Air (est.). The trawler should come in under that and the steamer about at it. The Titanic, with about 60 cells and far more openings, is likely several seconds (est. 3–9 s), measured at SH34. A match multiplies one bake by its must-sink bakes (5b.1: est. 1.0–1.7 on average, five at most). Every budget in a gate is an estimate to measure. The web build is single-threaded and slower (R20), so the bakes run during the menu and the countdown, sliced across frames. Because the must-sink rule accepts a hit only once its bake has finished, a match cannot start while its bake is still running: one that would overrun holds the countdown with a progress bar instead of baking ahead during play. Online the browser never bakes (D11).

The brawl

The verbs, and the triangle they make

  • Move. Analog, relative to where you look, 5 m/s, quick to start and stop. Your facing is your look (first person, D14), turned by the mouse or the right stick; bots turn at their profile's rate.
  • Shove (SH1). 3 ticks of windup, 3 active, 8 of recovery. A 40° cone in front, 0.9 m beyond the two bodies. A hit sends the target sliding at 8 m/s and staggers it for 0.4 s: no control, no grip. The windup is too short to react to — the counter to a quick shove is spacing and reads, not reflexes.
  • Charged shove (SH4, Q2). Hold shove past 0.2 s: you walk slowly and glow; release for an 11 m/s push that ignores a brace. Readable, punishable, costs stamina.
  • Brace (SH4, Q2). Hold to plant: rooted, and a shove into your front arc loses 75% of its force. It also holds you on a steep deck. Drains stamina.

Knockback rules

  • Direction is where the shover looks at the start of the active window (its facing, D14); at windup start the shove — never the view — bends toward the nearest target inside a 30° cone, the same for everyone.
  • A staggered body slides with low friction (8 m/s²): a quick shove on a flat deck moves a body about 3 m. Hitting a staggered body adds 25% (a combo).
  • Slopes add to it. Gravity in ship space pulls a sliding body downhill, so a shove downhill travels farther than one uphill — on a listing ship, which way you shove matters.
  • The railing rule (SH2). A railing stops anything crossing it slower than 7 m/s. At or above that, the body tips over it and falls. On a flat deck a quick shove sends you over only if you were pinned within about 1 m of the rail; a charged shove from about 4.5 m; downhill, farther.
  • Bodies push bodies. Circles separate; a fast body hitting another hands over part of its speed (restitution 0.2) — shoving into a crowd is a bowling ball.
  • The shover rocks back 1.5 m/s on a hit, and from SH9 both freeze for a 3-tick hit-stop — a rule, not an effect (D12).

Hazards, later — and the shape they must take

Bots, camera and controls

Bots

  • Decision model: utility intents, then steering. Every think_period ticks the brain scores a handful of intents and keeps the best (with hysteresis, so it does not dither): seek high ground, line up a shove (approach so the target sits between you and the nearest water or unrailed edge — the one skill that separates good brawlers), evade or brace against a facing, winding-up opponent, recover uphill when near an edge on a slope, guard the edge against a climber (SH5), climb back when swimming. Every tick in between it steers toward the intent's goal and emits an InputFrame.
  • Perception. A BotView built from the snapshot reaction_ticks ago: every body's position, velocity, state and facing, the current pose, which surfaces are dry, the distance to the waterline. No fog on an open deck, no future schedule (D10). Rooms change this (rev 3): until SH7 adds line of sight and hearing (Q16), bots see through walls a first-person player cannot (R16). Rev 4 adds each cell's water and air, which way is down, the lights and the openings' states — the present only (D10, §5b.3).
  • Navigation. From SH3, a coarse walk graph built from the layout: platforms are nodes, ramps are edges; the brain paths to a platform, then steers locally. From SH3b the graph's zones are rooms and open-deck areas joined by doorways and stairs, so paths go through doors, never walls. From SH32 (rev 4) it is rebuilt from the faces that are floors at the ship's attitude, and a shut watertight door closes its edge.
  • How many. Eight seats per match by default — you plus seven bots (Q4). The sim is O(n²) in body pairs with n ≤ 16, which is nothing; SH7 records the measured cost per tick and the budget is ≤ 2 ms for sim + bots at eight seats on the MacBook Air, with 16 seats checked for headroom.

Authority, prediction, hosting

  • Authority: a headless Godot (--headless -- --server, exported in the editor's dedicated-server mode) hosts rooms; each room is one MatchRunner at 30 Hz. Clients only ever send inputs; the server clamps them (D3) — that is the whole anti-cheat.
  • Prediction: the client runs ahead of the server by half its round trip plus a small buffer, so its frame for tick T arrives before T is stepped. It predicts the whole world with remote seats repeating their last input, but draws only its own seat from the prediction. A correction is smoothed over ~100 ms in the scene (D12).
  • Lag compensation (SH13) only if SH12's soak shows shoves that "should have hit": the server tests a shove against target positions rewound to what the shover saw, capped at 150 ms — possible because ShoveResolver takes positions as data (D13).
  • Hosting: the mini PC's established pattern — a compose stack (server container + static web build + cloudflared), .env.example, bin/deploy, a deploy job on the per-repo self-hosted runner. The web export stays single-threaded (the default), so no special COOP/COEP headers are needed.
  • Capacity: a quantized snapshot is ≈ 220 bytes at eight seats; at 30 Hz that is ≈ 6.6 kB/s per client, ≈ 0.4 Mbit/s of upload for a full room. A home line carries several rooms. These are the MVP's eight-seat sums; a Titanic-sized room needs interest management and delta snapshots (SH22). Rev 4 adds one transfer per match: the sinking timeline, streamed ahead of the tick (est. ≤ 200 kB on the steamer, ≤ 2 MB on the Titanic, measured in SH28 and SH34).

What SH1–SH10 must do so online is never a rewrite

  • The sim is Node-free and clock-free, enforced by check from SH1 (D1, D2).
  • Every control enters as a quantized InputFrame; the sim never knows who is local (D3).
  • from_snapshot(snapshot()) continues bit-identically, tested on every PR (D5).
  • Presentation reads snapshots only and interpolates two of them (D5).
  • The ship's pose is derived from the match's data and seed, never stored or mutated by play (D7) — from rev 4 through the sinking timeline, which the host bakes once and sends with its hash; a client never recomputes it.
  • Bots are seats behind an InputSource, never special-cased in the sim (D10).
  • ShoveResolver takes candidate positions as data (D13).
  • MatchConfig carries a hash of the data files the match loaded, and the InputLog records it — later a refused handshake, today a replay that refuses to run against changed numbers.

Risks

R1 · high — The brawl does not feel good

Hand-rolled kinematics can feel like ice or like mud, and no test catches it. Mitigation: every feel number is data (§6), so tuning is a .tres edit; SH1 is playable on day one, so feel is judged early and every milestone; SH9 is a milestone devoted to it; hit-stop and recoil are rules, so they survive the network. Each milestone's PR body carries one sentence on how it played from whoever ran make run.

R2 · high — A field the snapshot forgot

Add state to PlayerState but not to the snapshot and offline play still works — and online prediction silently diverges months later. Mitigation: the continuation test from SH1 (D5), run over the golden match and over a scripted scenario for each mechanic as it lands (stamina in SH4, the cold meter in SH5, hit-stop in SH9, crates in SH10), because a field the golden match never exercises escapes a test that only replays the golden match.

R3 · high — The endgame stalls into camping

The genre's oldest failure: the last three hold the best spot and wait. Mitigation: §5's seven mechanisms, led by the physics of a ship sinking by the head moving the high ground; SH7's arena measures it (≤ 10% of matches reach the plunge with three or more dry); every lever is data — event times, the grip angle, the collapse. Rev 4 removes the scripted levers (event times, the timed collapse, the cap): what stays is the physics moving the high ground any way, physical lurches, the funnel, and the second route the lint demands from SH26. She always sinks (Q18), so no match is camped on a ship that stays up. A wreck at rest with dry footing is different: there the match goes on until one player is left, with no limit (your answer to Q21), so players who keep apart on her can stall it, and the input log has no bound there. Mitigation, not a rule: the coast scenario's depth is data and sets how much of her stays dry; the lint's second routes; bots that hunt the last seats; the arena reports how long dry-wreck matches run, and the tools' STOP= keeps every test finite. No time limit is added. The arena and the census keep measuring it.

R4 · high — Agents cannot playtest

The most important property of a real-time game is the one an agent cannot see. Mitigation: every rule is in core/ under GUT; SH1's instruments (make match, make capture, --autoplay) give headless or one-shot evidence; SH7 turns "is the endgame the right shape" into numbers; the make run gates are explicitly for you or the QA agent, and the review reads the PR's play note.

R5 · high — Genre gravity

Battle royales attract loot, skins, progression and ranked play. Mitigation: D9 and §13 fence it; anything on that list is a new plan, never a milestone's extra.

R6 · medium — Float drift between platforms

Vector3 is single precision and the engine's maths is compiled per platform (fused multiply-add, the C library's sin/cos), so a match recorded on the arm64 Mac may not reproduce bit for bit on CI's x86-64 Linux, and one differing bit can grow through a collision branch. Mitigation: D4 promises same build, same platform only; goldens are per platform and a missing one is pending, not failing; the twice-run, log-replay and continuation tests run everywhere and catch the real defects (an unseeded roll, hidden state, an order dependence). Online play never needed cross-platform identity (D11).

R7 · medium — GDScript is too slow for sim + bots + prediction

Eight bodies at 30 Hz are cheap; bots' scoring and SH11's re-simulation of several ticks per snapshot multiply it. Budget: sim + bots ≤ 2 ms per tick at eight seats, measured in SH7 and again in SH11. Fallbacks, in order: a slower think rate for easy and normal bots; caching per-tick geometry queries; early-outs on distant pairs; last, a GDExtension for the hot loop — a plan decision with a toolchain cost, never a milestone's quiet fix.

R8 · medium — WebSocket's head-of-line stalls

Over TCP, one lost segment holds up everything behind it. Mitigation: redundant inputs and full snapshots mean a stall costs latency, never correctness; the Transport interface keeps the swap cheap; SH12 measures it; WebRTC data channels (browsers) and ENet (native) are Q8's fallbacks.

R9 · medium — A game server at home

Upload, uptime, a home machine on the internet. Mitigation: Cloudflare Tunnel (no open ports), room codes, clamped inputs, no accounts, a per-address join limit; §8's arithmetic says a room is about 0.4 Mbit/s of upload. If it outgrows the mini PC, the same container moves to a rented server unchanged.

R10 · medium — First person in greybox does not read

What is beside or behind you, how far a drop is, which way is downhill, where the water is when you are inside. Mitigation (rev 3): the inclinometer and the height-above-the-sea readout (SH9), off-screen windup arcs (SH9) and positional footsteps (SH15), seat colour, hat and an in-sight chevron on every brawler, a level horizon the tilt reads against (Q17), and blob shadows under others. Nothing is drawn through walls — the silhouette idea is retired with the elevated camera.

R11 · low — The editor helper autoload in exported builds

The scaffold registers _mcp_game_helper (godot_ai) as an autoload. Grid Commanders' mobile plan measured on Godot 4.7.1 that a feature-tagged override does not clear an autoload, and that excluding the addon while an entry still names it is a boot error. Mitigation: SH12 settles it once, for the server and the web export: the plugin registers its runtime singleton itself (Grid Commanders' recorded fallback), or the helper ships — measured, not assumed.

R12 · low — Two water authorities

The scaffold's WaterLevel would be a second answer to "how high is the water", in seconds rather than ticks. SH1 deletes it with its test (D7, D13).

R13 · high — The destination eats the MVP

A Titanic-sized goal pulls work forward before the small game is fun. Mitigation: SH16–SH35 start only after SH15; the MVP's changes for the destination are wording and tests only (the audit in §3); no MVP gate waits on a destination system.

R14 · medium — A huge ship is mostly empty deck

Scale can dilute the brawl into a walk. Mitigation: seats (Q12), pace (Q13; from rev 4, Q20 — deferred, real time for now) and spawn spread are data; SH20's arena reports idle time and meetings per minute on the big ship before anything is tuned.

R15 · high — Motion sickness on a listing ship

First person on a deck that tilts for minutes, plus lurches and view kicks, is the textbook trigger. Mitigation: a level horizon by default (Q17), no head-bob, a field-of-view slider (75–110°), shakes and kicks short and small (SH9b), the deck-roll slider at 0 by default; every milestone's play note says whether anyone felt it.

R16 · high — Omniscient bots against a blind player

Bots read the whole delayed snapshot — through walls and behind you — while a first-person player sees about 90°. Mitigation: perception already lags; SH7 filters BotView by line of sight plus 6 m hearing (Q16); the arena reports the win-share gap between sighted and omniscient bots, so the price of fairness is a number.

R17 · medium — Authoring rooms by hand

Walls, doors and stairs typed as rectangles into a .tres are slow and easy to get wrong. Mitigation: SH3b's lint (doors, no sealed room, two stairs up, nothing crossing a stair) catches the wrong; the steamer has about a dozen rooms, which is hand-sized. Rule: if SH3b's PR reports hand-authoring as its bottleneck, SH17's bake tool moves into the MVP before more rooms are added — decided then, not pre-paid now.

R18 · low — Performance inside rooms

Fine, and said so: walls add roughly fifty box blockers, so collision stays a few hundred cheap checks per tick at eight seats, and a lit interior with a lamp per room is light work for gl_compatibility on the Air. SH14b measures frame time on the flooding lower deck so "fine" is a number.

R19 · medium — A shove's windup is hard to read in first person

You only see what is in front of you, and a quick shove's windup is 100 ms. Mitigation: the windup pose reads from the front and the side (SH14); off-screen arcs (SH9) and a positional grunt and whoosh (SH15) cover the rest; the crosshair says when your own shove would land. The quick shove was always meant to be beaten by spacing and reads; the charged shove's glow and sound are the tells that matter.

R20 · high — A bake too slow for the web build rev 4

The web export is single-threaded and runs GDScript slower than the desktop (unmeasured); a bake of several seconds on the Titanic, times the must-sink rule's bakes (5b.4, est.), would freeze a browser tab. Mitigation: bake during the menu and the countdown, sliced across frames; a hit is accepted only once its bake has finished, so an overrun holds the countdown rather than baking ahead during play; online the host bakes and the browser only receives (D11). R21 · high — Float differences across platforms rev 4

A threshold — a door bursting, a capsize, a break — can land a step apart on a Mac, on Linux and in a browser, and D4 promises one build and platform only. Mitigation: online, clients play the host's timeline by its hash and never bake; offline goldens stay per platform; inside the bake only +, −, ×, ÷ and square roots on 64-bit floats, never Vector3, and rotations updated without sine or cosine; outcome tests assert labels and bands on seeds pinned far from thresholds, with the ±10% margin check, so CI on Linux and the Mac agree.

R22 · medium — Numerical chatter rev 4

Square-root flow at a long step never settles between two cells (the research measured ±0.36 m at a 30 s step); full cells and trapped air are stiff. Mitigation: every transfer capped at the levelling amount, a fixed sweep that reverses each step, the step landing on sills and ceilings, the pocket's level solved each step, an implicit attitude step; layer-1 tests assert no sloshing at a 30 s step.

R23 · high — Free surface capsizes too many ships rev 4

Correct free-surface physics in wide cells can roll every small ship over (a hobby game, scuttle, removed its model for that); one half-flooded full-width compartment costs the steamer about as much stability as she has (est.). Q18 · If the ship stays afloat, what ends the match? rev 4 · your decision (a)

decided · she always sinks · 2026-10-05

Decided by you on 2026-10-05: “the ship can't stay afloat”. No match is played on a ship that survives her hit. The physics still can leave a light hit afloat, and the tests keep that as validation; a match's hit is drawn again until she founders, within a bound and with a fixed fallback (§5b.1, from SH26). A match ends by the brawl or, once she is gone, by the cold. This reverses, for matches, the last sentence of your request, which the given in §3 keeps as you wrote it.

It left one case open — a wreck that comes to rest with part of her dry for good, on her side on the bottom in shallow water — which you answered as Q21: the match goes on until one player is left.

The question as rev 4 put it, with its options, kept for the record

From SH26 a light hit can leave the ship afloat (§5b), and today the only end besides the brawl is the script's cap, which rev 4 removes. How often it applies is set by the hit's bands: the census bands in §5b.4 put it at about a fifth to two-fifths of matches (est.). No option is pre-selected.

  • The brawl alone
    In play: an afloat ship stays a brawl until one seat is left dry; she may list or settle and stay that way; nothing else ends it.
    Costs: nothing guarantees an end — two careful players, or sparring bots, can hold out; tests and online rooms still need a safety limit, so test_match_always_ends becomes a measured distribution under that limit; the arena reports how long afloat matches run.
    Changes: no decision; SH26's end rule; R3 (camping) grows on an afloat ship.
  • A time limit for the match
    In play: a limit per ship in data; when it runs out the ship counts as rescued and every seat still dry places by its height above the water (ties by cold meter); players see it coming and can play for it.
    Costs: a clock comes back — on the match, not on the sinking; one number per ship to tune; whether the HUD shows the time left is a small call inside SH26.
    Changes: Q5 becomes this limit's value; SH26's end rule; test_match_always_ends keeps a hard bound.
  • The sinking's own end
    In play: every timeline ends in a state — gone, aground, or afloat with the water stopped; the match ends at that moment (in match time, through Q20's clock) and everyone still dry then shares the win; if she is gone, today's rule settles the swimmers by the cold.
    Costs: that moment can come long after the brawl would have ended — hours in, if Q20 keeps real time; a shared win is new (today a tie is a draw) and the results screen must show it.
    Changes: D7 (the timeline's end is the match's end); SinkSchedule.cap_tick becomes that end; tied to Q20; SH26, SH28.
  • Lifeboats are a way out
    In play: from the hit, boats on a side still usable can be boarded at their davits; a seat that boards one leaves the match safe and places above everyone who ends in the sea; an afloat match ends when the boats are full or gone, or one seat is left. A heavy list then matters in play: one side's boats are lost.
    Costs: a new rule and place on every ship (boarding zones, capacities), a bot intent, a results screen with two kinds of survivor; it changes the goal you gave ("last one standing on the highest dry spot wins"), so it amends your given, not just a rule.
